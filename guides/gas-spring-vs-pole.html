Gas-spring arm or fixed pole? Choose the movement you need

The useful difference is not a label. It is how often the screen needs to move and which limits the mechanism places around that routine.

A mechanism is a routine decision

A pole-based arm gives the desk a vertical reference. A screen can be positioned along that structure, while the articulated arm handles reach, tilt and rotation according to the model. This can suit a workspace where the display is set once and moved occasionally. The word “fixed” should not be taken to mean immovable; it means the height method is different from a lift mechanism.

A gas-spring or constant-force arm uses a counterbalance mechanism to support a stated load range while the user repositions the display. The appeal is frequent adjustment: sit, stand, share a screen or clear the desk. The tradeoff is that the mechanism has a meaningful minimum as well as a maximum, and the adjustment depends on correct tension and the actual mounted load.

Use the VIVO and LX as documented examples

VIVO STAND-V001 is a useful example of a compact single-monitor arm built around a pole. Its manufacturer lists a 22 lb maximum, 75 × 75 and 100 × 100 VESA support, a 17-inch pole height and 16-inch maximum extension. Those dimensions do not describe a continuous lift range, and the product page does not establish a minimum monitor load or every minimum desk-clamp condition.

Ergotron LX 45-241-224 is a useful example of a lift-oriented arm. Its manufacturer data lists 7–25 lb, 13 inches of lift, 75/100 VESA, a typical screen size up to 34 inches and a 10-year warranty. The dimensions note reduced lower lift above 20 lb, and depth above 2.5 inches may diminish capacity. This is a documented specification comparison, not a hands-on judgment about which feels smoother.

Why minimum load matters

A light display can be outside a lift mechanism’s range even when it is far below the maximum. If the mechanism expects a load and receives less, the arm may not hold the intended position without adjustment. Do not solve that by adding improvised weight or by treating resistance as a test. Check the manual’s adjustment process and ask the manufacturer whether the exact screen configuration is supported.

A pole-based design may avoid that particular counterbalance question, but it still has load, attachment and reach limits. Every mechanism needs a desk that can carry its base and a VESA connection that matches the screen. The mechanism changes the questions; it does not remove them.

Choose by calendar, not by brochure

Write down how often the screen moves. “Once a week” is a different routine from “every time I change between writing and a call.” If the display needs to move vertically several times a day, a documented lift range may be relevant. If it mostly stays in one place and the desk is tight, a pole arrangement may be easier to reason about. That is a selection hypothesis, not a performance claim.

Then test the path on paper. Mark the display’s highest and lowest useful positions, the wall distance, the arm elbow and the cable slack. A mechanism that can move in theory may not move through the path the desk provides. Use the manufacturer’s drawings and the supplied manual for the final configuration.

A mechanism-neutral checklist

  1. Identify the exact mechanism and model number, including clamp version.
  2. Compare the loaded monitor against both minimum and maximum limits.
  3. Record the usable height range separately from pole height or maximum reach.
  4. Check screen depth, desk contact and clearance at the intended positions.
  5. Follow the manufacturer’s tension and installation instructions without inventing a universal setting.
