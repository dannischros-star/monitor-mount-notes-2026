Monitor height and viewing distance: a calmer starting point

A comfortable monitor position is a relationship between your eyes, the screen, the desk and the light in the room. A single number cannot settle it.

Start with the screen, not the arm

A monitor arm is a positioning tool. It does not create a good viewing position by itself. Before choosing a pole height or a spring range, sit in the position you use for the longest uninterrupted part of the day and look straight ahead. Notice where your eyes naturally rest, how far the screen sits from your face, and whether you are leaning forward to read. Then repeat the observation while standing if the desk changes height.

OSHA’s computer-workstation monitor guidance is a useful baseline: keep the display directly in front of you, put the top of the screen at or below eye level, and maintain at least 20 inches of viewing distance. Treat that 20-inch figure as a minimum reference rather than a promise of comfort. Larger text, a deeper screen, a different prescription or a particular visual need may call for a different distance. The practical test is whether you can read without craning your neck or moving your whole torso.

Height is about the top edge and the line of sight

People often describe a monitor as “eye level” when they mean the screen center is at eye level. That is not the same arrangement. For a conventional desktop screen, placing the top edge at or slightly below eye level often lets the eyes look gently downward toward the middle of the display. A tall monitor, a large text scale or a screen used in portrait orientation changes the relationship. Measure the screen itself, not the mounting plate or the top of the arm pole.

A mount’s published pole height is not a usable screen-height range. VIVO STAND-V001’s 17-inch pole height describes the pole component; it does not state where every screen center will land. Ergotron’s LX documentation describes a 13-inch lift range for the exact family of arm, but the available position still depends on the base, arm geometry, monitor dimensions and load. Use the manufacturer drawing to check a real screen position.

Distance, angle and glare work together

Distance and angle should be considered together. A screen that is too close may force frequent refocusing or make the user rotate the neck to see the edges. A screen that is too far may tempt the user to increase brightness or lean toward the text. A slight backward tilt can help a screen face the user rather than the ceiling, but an aggressive tilt can introduce reflections or make the bottom edge harder to read.

Windows and bright lamps can turn a nominally correct position into a tiring one. OSHA’s monitor guidance calls attention to glare and window placement. Start by changing the screen’s relationship to the light source: rotate the desk or display, close a shade, or reposition a lamp before simply increasing brightness. Glossy surfaces and high contrast reflections are room problems as much as monitor problems. Your final setup should remain readable at the time of day when you actually work.

Seated and standing positions

A sit-stand desk needs two positions that both work. Record the eye height seated and standing, then compare the difference with the arm’s documented lift or with the height you can achieve by changing the desk. If the monitor remains too low in standing mode, the user may tilt the head down for long periods. If it is too high while seated, shoulders and neck may stiffen as the user looks up.

Make the transition path part of the check. The display, arm elbow and cables all move while the desk rises. The right static height can still be a poor dynamic fit if a wall, shelf or lamp blocks the arc. A monitor arm should let the user move between positions without forcing a collision or a cable pull. See the standing-desk guide for a separate cable and clearance worksheet.

Accessibility is a real constraint

Comfort guidance is not a substitute for an individual accessibility need. A user may need a screen lower, closer, farther, tilted differently or paired with magnification, a screen reader or a specialized input device. A tall monitor may need different placement from a short one. If a standard eye-level recommendation conflicts with a clinician’s instruction or the user’s functional needs, follow the appropriate professional guidance and verify that the arm can hold the resulting configuration.

Use OSHA’s baseline as a starting point, then adjust for the person, the display and the room. Record the chosen position so it can be reproduced after cleaning, moving the desk or replacing the monitor.

A five-minute position check

  1. Sit or stand in the intended working posture and mark the natural eye height.
  2. Measure from your eyes to the front of the screen; keep the manufacturer’s minimum distance in view.
  3. Place the screen directly ahead, then check the top edge, text size and viewing angle.
  4. Look for reflections at the actual time and lighting condition of work.
  5. Move the display through its intended adjustment range and check the wall, desk and cable path.
